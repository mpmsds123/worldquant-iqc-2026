The competition runs in stages: an open qualifying window, then a scored window on the field that carries forward. Alphas are submitted to a shared pool and scored as a basket, in-sample over 2019–2023 and out-of-sample from 2024, with credit for returns that other people's alphas do not already have.

Between my first submission window and my second, my competition score went from 2,270 to 9,374. What follows is the reconstruction of why — from the 36 alphas themselves, their day-by-day simulated PnL, and the search that produced them.

The scoring

The platform turns those numbers into dollar-neutral position weights, simulates them on a fixed $20M book from 2019 to 2023, and reports the usual statistics. The one identity worth memorising

Ranking is driven by fitness, not Sharpe, and fitness is not a free parameter — it is this:

fitness = Sharpe × √( |returns| / max(turnover, 0.125) )

I checked it against all 36 of my own submissions rather than trusting the documentation: maximum absolute error — across the set, which is rounding. Read it and the strategy falls out. Turnover sits under a square root and is floored at 0.125, so cutting turnover from 0.50 to 0.125 multiplies fitness by 2, and cutting it below 0.125 buys nothing at all. Returns also sit under the square root. Sharpe is the only term that is linear. But Sharpe is also the term every competitor is already grinding on, which makes it the most expensive place to look for the next 10%.

Where the alphas came from

Four sources, one filter

Every scanner batch assigned to the source it was actually written from

Candidate expressions came from four places. Paper replication — taking a published finding and restating it as an expression, which is a translation problem more than a coding one. Dataset sweeps, where I read one dataset's field documentation end to end and then swept its fields through a template. An LLM research pipeline built on an MCP server that hands a model the simulation API, a vector index of the platform's own documentation and an arXiv search tool, and has it propose expressions against a named dataset. And systematic expansion, crossing operators with fields with no prior about which combinations should work.

All four feed the same filter. An expression is only a candidate once it clears the submission gate, and only worth a slot once it is also uncorrelated with what I already hold.

The ordering is the useful part, and it is not the one I expected. Reading a dataset properly beat everything. Systematic expansion consumed more compute than the other three combined and returned the second-worst rate. And replication came last — a published effect is published, so by the time it reaches a shared alpha pool it is both well known and heavily correlated with what everyone else has already submitted.

What replication is actually for is the conclusion: the claim a paper makes about why a price should move. Copying its formula reproduces a crowded factor. Taking its claim and re-expressing it — different field, different horizon, a condition the original did not have — produces something the pool has not already priced. The highest-fitness alpha in this book began as a published result about option-implied information and only worked once it was gated on positioning and given a horizon of its own.

That translation step is where the operators earn their keep. The five expressions below use the same small vocabulary throughout:

  • vector_neut(signal, exposure) — project out the risk factor a signal is secretly loading on, so what is left is the idea rather than the exposure.
  • group_rank(x, bucket(rank(cap), …)) — compare a company against genuine peers instead of the whole universe, which is what stops a ratio from becoming a size bet.
  • trade_when(condition, x, -1) — hold no position when the condition fails, rather than taking the opposite side of a signal that is simply absent.
  • hump(x, threshold) — refuse to trade on noise-level readings; the single cheapest way to pull turnover down without touching the signal.
  • ts_backfill, winsorize(std=4), quantile(driver=gaussian) — make a sparse, fat-tailed field usable at all before asking whether it predicts anything.
The mechanism

The score is not won on Sharpe

Median of the first 10 submissions versus median of the next 26

The clearest evidence sits in my own two submission windows. Between them the median Sharpe of what I submitted went down, and the competition score went up four-fold. Every term that moved in the right direction is a term other people were not competing over.

Turnover is the headline: the median alpha went from turning over 12.4% of the book a day to 7.3%. Because turnover enters fitness under a square root, that alone is worth about a third more fitness at unchanged Sharpe and returns. Margin — profit per dollar traded — rose 52%, which is the same fact from the other side: an edge that survives being traded less often is a real edge, and one that needs constant churn usually is not.

The second change does not show up in any per-alpha statistic at all. The first ten alphas drew on seven data families; the next twenty-six drew on nineteen, and eighteen of the twenty-six use a family the first ten never touched. Median self-correlation against my own existing submissions fell from 0.36 to 0.30. The competition scores a basket, and a basket pays more for an alpha that is merely good and unlike the others than for one that is excellent and duplicated.

What transfers

Four things I would tell anyone starting this

  • Derive the objective before optimising anything. Fitness is Sharpe × √(|returns| / max(turnover, 0.125)). Turnover and returns sit under a square root; turnover has a floor that tells you exactly where the payoff stops. Sharpe is the only linear term, and it is also the one every other competitor is already grinding on — which makes it the most expensive place to look for the next ten percent.
  • Aim the search at a dataset, not at the operator space. Sweeps written against documentation I had actually read cleared the gate at 1.86%; crossing operators with fields blindly cleared it at 0.48%, having consumed nearly three fifths of all the compute. Compute is not the constraint; knowing what a field means is.
  • Treat correlation as a design constraint, not a check to pass. An alpha that duplicates one you already hold adds almost nothing to a basket, however good it looks alone. The slot is better spent on a dataset you have never opened.
  • Orthogonalize on purpose. Several signals here are really volatility or size bets in disguise until the exposure is explicitly removed — ranking inside cap buckets, neutralizing on subindustry, projecting out realized volatility. That step usually costs raw Sharpe and is usually still worth it.

Provenance. Every statistic on this page is derived from data pulled from the WorldQuant BRAIN API for my own account — the 36 submitted alpha records with their simulation settings, in-sample statistics and check results, and the daily PnL recordset for each. Ranks and field sizes are from the competition leaderboards. The funnel counts come from 34 local scanner log files plus a day-by-day count of simulations from the API. Expressions for the five featured alphas are reproduced exactly as submitted; the remaining thirty-one are summarised in aggregate only. The page is generated by build.py from those files: every figure in the tiles, charts, captions and tables is computed at build time. Figures quoted in the running prose are those same computed numbers, restated.

Caveats. All simulation statistics are in-sample over 2019–2023 on the platform's USA equity universe, and the alphas were selected on that same window. The platform's PnL recordset carries no entry for March 2020, so that month is absent from every curve here. Nothing on this page is a live track record.
